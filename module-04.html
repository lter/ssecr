Searching for Consensus & Data

Your group has now likely begun to meet regularly and some of the technical work has likely also begun. You may now benefit from discussing tools for enabling full participation, as well as some models for thinking about how we communicate in groups and how some patterns of “stuck” communication can be resolved. Finally, we’ll end on a primer for finding data useful for your project, either from existing data repositories or via sensibly-composed, general searches.

Moving from Debate to Dialogue

Tip Learning Objectives

After completing this topic you will be able to:

  • Paraphrase the difference between “debate” and “dialogue”
  • Identify the four actions of effective communication in David Kantor’s model

Dialogue is a collaborative effort to understand and learn from each other. Debate, on the other hand, is an oppositional effort to convince the other side that you are right. Inclusive facilitation aims to support dialogue and skillful discussion. Dialogue allows groups to recognize the limits on their own and others’ individual perspectives and to strive for more coherent thought. Dialogue becomes a container for collective thinking and exploration–a process that can take teams in directions not imagined or planned.

In dialogue, all views are treated as equally valid, and different views are presented as a means toward discovering a new view. Participants listen to understand one another, not to win. Complex issues are explored and shared meaning is created. When it comes time to make a decision, skillful discussion is required. Both skillful discussion and dialogue are critical to the collaborative process, and the more artfully a group can move between these two forms of discourse (and out of less productive debate and polite discussion) according to what is needed, the more effective the group will be.

David Kantor’s Four Player Model is a helpful tool for diagnosing “stuck” patterns of communication, (including entrenched debate and polite discussion), and interceding to help shift to a more productive pattern.

Based on over 30 years of observation and study of face-to-face communication in many groups, Kantor developed the Four Player Model and a broader theory of Structural Dynamics. The model identifies four actions of effective communication:

  • Move - Initiate an idea, action, or direction for conversation
  • Follow - Continue the direction or flow of the conversation; support a move, either by agreeing or asking for more information
  • Oppose - Challenge or disagree with the idea, action, or suggested direction
  • Bystand - Notice and articulate what’s happening in the conversation, add a neutral perspective

Each action in a group conversation can be coded into one of these four action modes. Most of us have one of these modes that we feel most comfortable in and tend to default to in a group. The most effective conversations involve good listening and the skillful use of all four modes. Common “stuck” patterns emerge when groups are not deploying all four actions.

  • Serial Moves
  • What to Do

Lots of idea generation; may feel like a barrage; no clear thread, decision, or follow through

Any of the other modes can help here, since move is the only mode being engaged.

Add a follow to give momentum to a particular move and steer the conversation in that direction, e.g., “Can we go back to the idea that Jose put on the table? That felt like a topic that could really use our attention. Shall we focus there?”

Offer an effective oppose - “We’ve heard a lot of different ideas. I’d like to focus on the one Amelia laid out. I’m interested in the research question, but I don’t think machine learning is going to be the most productive approach. Can we dig in to this one?”

Use a bystand to bring awareness to and disrupt the dynamic - “Hey gang, we’re 20 minutes into our call and we’ve put a lot of different topics on the table. Where do we want to focus ourselves so we can walk away with some clear next steps?”

  • Polite Discussion
  • What to Do

Moves are followed with little discussion or resistance; also known as Courteous Compliance

Prompt an effective oppose:

  • Who sees it differently?
  • What’s at risk here?
  • What other angles should we consider?

Invite a bystand:

  • Where is the group right now?
  • What are you noticing?
  • Is there an elephant in the room that needs to be named?
  • Point-Counterpoint / Debate
  • What to Do

Individuals are locked in a back and forth where each move is met with resistance / opposition

Invite a follow:

  • What do you like about the proposal on the table?
  • What do you agree with that we could build upon?

Coach for a more effective oppose by inviting those who have been opposing to identify some aspect of the idea they do agree with (even if only 2%), in addition to the specific aspects they object to.

Invite a bystand:

  • In addition to the two viewpoints on the table, I’d love to hear from some other perspectives.
  • What are you noticing?
  • What might we be missing?
  • Covert Opposition
  • What to Do

On the surface, moves are followed, and the conversation appears harmonious, but below the surface, people have unspoken reservations. Opposition tends to be expressed outside the bounds of the conversation or harbored as resentment.

Uneven power dynamics are often behind this pattern - group members defer to the moves of those with more power or seniority.

Invite those with more power to experiment with following or bystanding to open up space for other players to make a move.

Prompt a transparent oppose:

  • Who sees it differently?
  • What’s at risk here?
  • Are there some cons to the proposed idea?

If others aren’t comfortable, you can offer an oppose, e.g., by suggesting the limits of the proposed move be tested across different scenarios.

Offer a bystand, e.g., “I want to offer a reflection from another team I was part of. On that team, we kept having meetings where it seemed like everyone was in agreement, but then we would leave, and over and over again there would be little follow through and more than a little grousing. People’s real opinions were only coming out in side conversations outside of the meeting. We lost a lot of time and forward momentum because people didn’t feel like they could air their concerns in the larger group. Do you see that happening here? Does anyone have a suggestion for how to make this a safer space to critically discuss ideas?”

Choosing Search Terms

Tip Learning Objectives

After completing this topic you will be able to:

  • Understand the concepts and terms that are useful when searching for research data
  • Refine your synthesis group’s research question into the terms of a data query

The answer to any research question is found in data. Many research questions are formulated with specific data in mind, but synthesis questions are often formulated with limited data in-hand and an intention to scale up the question to “all the available data.” Therefore, synthesis projects usually begin with a process of data discovery — finding datasets that can be used to answer the question.

Finding the data you need for synthesis isn’t always easy, but starting with a clear research question helps because you can map it to the criteria and search terms that you’ll use. This is because research questions and datasets both have the same conceptual elements, which include:

  • Observational units: a thing or phenomenon to be observed or measured. Examples: an ecosystem process, a layer of soil, animals, part of a tree
  • Variables: an attribute or characteristic of the observational units that can take different values, and which can be observed, measured, or estimated. Examples: precipitation, gas flux, species abundance

When mapping these concepts to data, you can think of the observational units as being represented by rows in a data table, and variables being represented by the columns. Other elements that will appear in both a research question and the data are:

  • Groups, which are observed or hypothesized subdivisions in the observational units and data. Examples: treatments, taxonomic group, functional types
  • Temporal dimensions, which are often necessary to understand when things happen or the effect of time. Examples: time of observation, age of subject
  • Spatial dimensions, which describe where things happen, or the effect of location. Examples: location of observation or study subject, distance to resource

Translating these concepts, as they are appear in your research question, to the attributes of the data you will search will help you find useful datasets more efficiently.

Some examples of mappings between research questions and data search terms.
Research question Conceptual mapping Data search criteria
Does species richness increase or decrease after disturbance? Does variable X increase or decrease with time?
  • Variables: presence/absence, richness (# species)
  • Obs. units: sampling area w/ known disturbance time
  • Groups: maybe?
  • Temporal: time since disturbance
Are forests or deserts more sensitive to vapor pressure deficit? Is group 1 or group 2 more strongly related to variable X?
  • Variables: VPD, NPP, evapotranspiration rate, etc.
  • Obs. units: site meteorology & ecosystem processes
  • Groups: forest ecosystems, desert ecosystems
  • Temporal: probably (processes vary over time)
Is the latitudinal gradient in small mammal body size changing? Is the relationship between variable 1 and variable 2 changing?
  • Variables: body length, mass, capture location
  • Obs. units: individual small mammals
  • Groups: species? functional group? year captured?
  • Temporal: yes, at least two time periods
  • Spatial: yes, need a latitude gradient

Data Repositories

Tip Learning Objectives

After completing this topic you will be able to:

  • Understand the differences between generalist repositories, more specialized repositories, and search aggregators.
  • Identify some repositories “known for” particular types of data

When you start a synthesis research project, you may already have some datasets in-hand at the outset but it is likely that you will need to find more data to test your hypotheses. Research data repositories are organizations or facilities that are primarily dedicated to storing and managing research data, so they are a great place to find and access relevant data for answering your research question. There are a lot of data repositories out there. In fact, the Registry of Research Data Repositories (re3data.org) lists 3526 repositories at the time of writing, so you probably can’t search them all exhaustively. It helps to know the landscape and search efficiently.

TipA word about metadata and searching repositories

We’re about describe a bunch of repositories, and then move on to strategies for searching them. It is first worth mentioning that, while we visit repositories to access the many, many data files they contain, every repository (so far as we know) also publishes metadata describing those data. If you’ve never heard of metadata before, the quick definition is “data about the data,” or information describing a unit of data. Metadata usually contains information about:

  • who collected the data
  • what was collected
  • when, where, how and why the data were collected.

When you search in a repository or aggregator, you are not searching the data themselves, you are searching the metadata. The metadata contain essential information that let you understand the data, evaluate if they are fit for your research purpose, and use the data. So, the essential unit of data you should think about during synthesis research is the dataset — data and metadata together. Keep the dataset concept in mind as you create your synthesis data products, and remember to collect metadata.

We can categorize repositories a few different ways. Generalist repositories hold all kinds of data from all sorts of research. They tend to have fairly lax requirements for metadata and data quality, so you may have to sift through your search results carefully to determine if a dataset is fit for use. Domain-specific repositories specialize in data from a particular domain, such as a scientific discipline (e.g. environmental science or biomedical research), location (e.g. Arctic data), or research network (e.g. NEON). They tend to have higher metadata standards for the data they accept into the repository. Some repositories are also data-specific, meaning they specialize in a limited number of data types and may have strict requirements for metadata and data formatting. These differences in repository specialization can have big impacts on how much time you spend finding useful data and getting it ready to analyze.

Below we present three non-exhaustive lists of repositories that have proven useful to other synthesis projects in the past. They are broken into categories as above, and are in alphabetical order. If the “ Package” column contains the GitHub logo () then the package is available on GitHub but is not available on CRAN (or not available at time of writing).

  • Specialized repositories
  • Generalist repositories

The Search, Evaluate, and Refine Loop

Tip Learning Objectives

After completing this topic you will be able to:

  • Explain some strategies you can use to effectively search for data in repositories, aggregators and search engines
  • Evaluate your data search results and judge whether they can be used to answer a research question

Searching for data effectively is an iterative process. You’ll usually start with simple exploratory searches, evaluate results, refine the search to include more specific terms or logic, and then search again. You may have to execute this loop multiple times to get the results you need for a synthesis project.

The evaluation step in this loop is key and typically involves answering some questions about the search results. These questions should range from simple sanity checks to in-depth evaluation of individual datasets, and the answers you get will tell you how to refine your search. Below are some examples of questions to ask during evaluation.

  • Did the search find datasets you know are present? This is a basic sanity check that tells you if your search terms are effective. If you started a synthesis project with some candidate datasets, or you’ve browsed the target repository a little, just make sure you can find things you already know are there.
  • After spot-checking a few search results, do they appear fit-for-use? “Fit-for-use” means a dataset can be used to answer your research question. To know this you must check whether the observational units, variables, groups, and temporal/spatial dimensions that you are interested in are present.
  • Did you get too many results to evaluate? If you did, you may want to add or exclude search terms and try the search again, or find another way to winnow down the results.
  • Did you get too few results? If your early searches return only a few results, or no results, you’ll probably need to expand your search terms or search in other repositories. If you consistently get few to no results when searching repositories try an even more targeted approach like reaching out directly to data managers and researchers.

Once you’ve done proper evaluation, you need to refine the search. How to do this depends on the answers to your evaluation questions above. In the tabs below we describe some practical strategies you can use to refine the next iteration of your search.

  • Expand search terms
  • Advanced queries
  • Winnow huge results
  • Search in new places

Dataset authors sometimes use heterogeneous terms for observational units, variables, groups and temporal/spatial dimensions when describing a dataset for publication. Repository curators do not always standardize these terms in the published metadata. So, if you didn’t find what you were hoping to find in your search, you may have already narrowed your results by choosing overly-specific search terms. Try adding synonyms and closely-related terms, or expanding the search to broader concepts and terms. Here are a few suggestions on how to do that:

  • The scientific terms used to name variables, observational units and groups often have commonly used synonyms and acronyms that are worth adding these to the search, e.g.
    • “soil respiration” and “soil CO2 efflux”
    • “net primary production” and “NPP”
    • “grasses” and “Poaceae”
  • Many search terms have closely-related concepts or variables that can be searched for, including variables derived or calculated from others.
    • “fertilization” and “N addition”
    • “primary production” and “carbon assimilation” and “photosynthesis”
    • “percent cover” and “community composition”
  • Searches can be made broader in a number of ways, including conceptually, taxonomically, temporally and spatially. Some suggestions:
    • Include metrics or indices derived from your variables of interest, such as “Simpson’s diversity index” (derived from species abundance data) or “vapor pressure deficit” (derived from air temperature and humidity).
    • Search broader taxonomic groups (genus or family) or functional groups (herbivores, benthic macroinvertebrates), instead of only species binomials.
    • Search terms for higher-level processes or phenomena can produce many related variables of interest. For example a “forest carbon cycle” search might produce “litterfall” results, and a “eutrophication” search might produce “dissolved oxygen” results.
    • Seach on region, state, or country names in addition to more localized site names. For instance, searching for the “North Atlantic Ocean” might produce useful results if “NES LTER” does not.

Constructing a more advanced query can help you either expand or narrow your search. Usually this is done using search “operators”. Virtually all search engines support operators to help users create more effective queries, and if you don’t use operators, most systems just return any results with matching words. With operators you can combine, narrow or expand terms, and include or exclude matches with logical operations. This helps hone search results when you have very specific criteria for candidate datasets.

Below are some useful operators that you can use with many search tools.

Quotes

Use quotation marks ("") to search for an exact phrase. This is particularly useful when you need specific data points or exact wording.

Example: "reef biodiversity"

Wildcard

Use an asterisk (*) to search using a placeholder for any word or phrase in the query. This is useful for finding variations of a term.

Example: Pinus * data

Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT)

Boolean operators let you introduce logic into the search by combining and excluding search terms in different ways. The Boolean operators below can be combined in different ways go create complex queries.

  • The ‘or’ operator (OR, |) means to search for either one term or another. It broadens your search to include multiple terms.
    • Example: "prairie pollinator" OR "grassland pollinator"
  • The ‘and’ operator (AND, &, +) means to search for one term and another at the same time. It narrows the search and is useful when you need combinations of criteria to be met.
    • Example: bat + cactus
  • The ‘not’ operator (NOT, -, !) means to exclude certain terms from your search. Useful to filter out irrelevant results.
    • Example: marine biodiversity data -fishery

Search indexed metadata

Most repository and aggregator search interfaces index specific metadata fields for search and expose them as operators that include those fields in search queries. Below are some examples from Google Dataset Search, but similar capabilities are available at other repositories and aggregators and should be available in the documentation.

  • Site: Use the site operator (site:) to search within a specific website or domain. This is helpful when you’re looking for data from a particular source. Example: site:.gov bird data
  • File Type: Use the file type operator (filetype:) to search for data with a specific file extension. Useful to make sure the data you find is already in a format you can interact with. Example: filetype:tif precipitation data
  • In Title: Use the ‘in title’ operator (intitle:) to search for pages that have a specific word in the title. This can narrow down your results to more relevant pages. Example: intitle:"lithology"
  • In URL: Use the ‘in URL’ operator (inurl:) to search for pages that have a specific word in the URL. This can help locate data repositories or specific datasets. Example: inurl:data soil chemistry

Sometimes searching for data yields large numbers of datasets, even after going a few times around the Search→Evaluate→Refine loop. This might be because there are plenty of research data about the topic, or it might be because the search tools and metadata are not adequate to filter the search results. Either way, large result sets can be very difficult and time-consuming to evaluate. If you wound up with a very large search result, you’ll want to systematically and carefully review the datasets to make sure that they are fit-for-use. Here are a few pointers:

  • Keep the original search result for reproducibility. You may want to return to it in the future.
  • Be clear about and document what critera are used to exclude datasets from the result set.
  • Distribute the work equitably. Reviewing datasets can be tedious and unrewarding, so don’t put it all on one person.
Caution Generative AI Considerations

Generative AI models can be helpful for long, repetitive tasks like extracting metadata from, or applying filtering criteria to, large search results. If you or your synthesis group has an unmanageably large search result to evaluate, delegating to genAI can be helpful if the group is comfortable with that. Make sure you follow some clear guidelines though:

  1. Having the agent or model download and ingest every dataset can over-burden the repository and consume too many tokens. Instead, consider downloading metadata first for the model to iterate over and review.
  2. Give the agent or model a clear plan to follow for each dataset.
  3. Save all prompts and model outputs to enable some reproducibility, and for disclosure purposes.

Sometimes, even after several times around the Search→Evaluate→Refine loop, you may come up with very few search results. At this time you should consider:

  1. Searching in more repositories. You can refer to the lists of generalist, domain-specific, and specialized repositories mentioned above for ideas on new places to search. As you search more sources, consider putting your searches into a script for reproducibility. Many repositories and aggregators have search APIs and wrappers that you can use for this.
  2. Reaching out to data managers and researchers for help. When you think there is data to be found but that it may not be published in repositories yet, it may be worth reaching out to a data manager or individual researchers. Many research sites and networks have dedicated data managers that can help connect you with data, or the researchers collecting it. Check websites for contact information.
Caution Generative AI Considerations

Generative AI models are skilled at understanding repository APIs and writing code to use them. If a repository provides a search API, using genAI to write search scripts that make API requests to the repository can be a great way to speed up your searches and make them more reproducible. There are two important considerations before you do this:

  1. Do you want to learn how to write API requests yourself?
  2. Will the GenAI code overburden the repository API by sending too many requests?

If the answer to these questions is “yes”, don’t take this shortcut without learning more and consulting with the repository first.

As you proceed through the Search→Evaluate→Refine loop, remember that reproducible science practices apply to search too! You should document your search process, just as you would document group decisions, analytical procedures, and other activities. For data search, be sure to keep track of:

  • What repositories, aggregators, or other data sources you searched
  • Your search queries and the results for each round of searching
  • How you filtered the results after the search
  • Datasets you found found that were fit for use (this will become a “Data Inventory,” as we’ll discuss later).
  • What data you didn’t find

Contingency plans

Sometimes, despite all the best efforts that a synthesis group makes, there isn’t much data to be found. This isn’t necessarily the end of the story for your synthesis question or project. A lack of data could mean that you have a truly novel research question, or that you have uncovered a “gap” in how the research community approaches certain topics. In such cases, the focus of the synthesis project can shift slightly away from finding, harmonizing and analyzing data and towards things like:

  • Writing a perspective piece highlighting the percieved gap in the research data
  • Proposing a new research network or program to answer a novel research question
